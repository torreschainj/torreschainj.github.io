  <h2>Working Papers</h2>

  <article class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">Family Care as Informal Insurance: Medicaid, Marriage, and Widowhood in Old Age</div>
    <div class="paper-status">Job Market Paper</div>
    <div class="paper-abstract"><strong>Abstract.</strong> This paper studies how family care changes the value of means-tested long-term-care insurance. I combine evidence from the Health and Retirement Study on care from spouses, children, other relatives, and paid providers with an estimated life-cycle model of singles, couples, and widowed survivors. The model links health and survival risk, family care, paid care, saving, and Medicaid receipt to separate two questions: how much insurance families already provide, and how much public insurance adds once that care is present. Within the estimated home-care model, a 10 percent increase in Medicaid's consumption floors is worth \$4,129 in age-70 assets when family care is available and \$4,911 when it is removed, so family care offsets 15.9 percent of the expansion's value. The offset is about twice as large for couples as for singles and widows. Removing family care costs recipient households \$39,588 in equivalent age-70 assets and raises paid care from 0.375 to 0.843 hours per day, yet reduces lifetime Medicaid transfers by only \$617. This wedge arises because family care protects consumption and assets before the means test binds. In a demographic scenario in which family care falls by about 35 percent by 2060, paid care replaces only 28 percent of the lost hours, leaving households to absorb roughly 70 percent. The results imply that family care is a large source of private insurance but a small source of fiscal savings.</div>
  </article>

  <article class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">Substitutes for Saving: Family Care, Long-Term-Care Risk, and Race in Late Life</div>
    <div class="paper-abstract"><strong>Abstract.</strong> At age 70, Black retirees hold about one fifth of the median wealth of White retirees, and the ratio barely moves until death. Why do such different circumstances—worse health, shorter lives, and twice the probability of an intensively caregiving family (23 versus 11 percent of single women)—produce such similar saving behavior? I estimate a life-cycle model of retirement saving with graded care needs, nursing-home risk, and ex-ante family types on Health and Retirement Study data and use it to answer the question in three steps. First, family care is insurance: intensive-care families provide up to ten hours of daily care, sharply reduce nursing-home risk, and have a markedly weaker estimated bequest motive, so their members hold less precautionary wealth at every income level. Second, initial conditions at retirement—wealth, health, and family composition—account for 86–95 percent of the racial wealth-path gap in an order-invariant decomposition; no behavioral channel accounts for more than about a tenth. Third, re-estimating preferences on Black moments shows patience and the valuation of care are the same across races; the residual that circumstances cannot explain is concentrated in access to the means-tested floor and in bequest behavior—both weakly identified as parameter levels, but pointing to institutions rather than tastes—not in patience or the value of care. The insurance role of the family gives policy a racial gradient: paying family caregivers is worth roughly twice as much to Black retirees, while equalizing nursing-home risk is worth most to White retirees.</div>
  </article>

  <article class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">The Incidence of Health Risk: Home Production, Self-Insurance, and Who Bears the Cost</div>
    <div class="paper-authors">with <a href="https://franciscobullano.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Francisco Bullano</a></div>
    <div class="paper-abstract"><strong>Abstract.</strong> We study who bears the welfare cost of health risk and whether home production provides self-insurance. Using matched panel data on consumption, time use, health, and wages, we show that large cross-sectional health gradients in wages and recovered home-production efficiency are largely driven by selection: within couples they nearly disappear, and neither changes at illness onset. We therefore classify couples by permanent income and estimate income-specific health processes in a life-cycle model. Low-income couples spend three times as many years in bad health and hold only one-quarter as much wealth as high-income couples. The model implies that health risk is three times more costly for low-income households, which cope mainly by drawing down assets rather than increasing home production.</div>
  </article>

  <article class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">Why do (most) households save and work?</div>
    <div class="paper-authors">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/site/margheritaborella/home" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Margherita Borella</a>, <a href="https://users.nber.org/~denardim/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Mariacristina De Nardi</a>, and <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/fangyang/home" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Fang Yang</a></div>
    <div class="paper-status">Revise and resubmit at the <em>American Economic Review</em></div>
    <div class="paper-links"><a href="https://users.nber.org/~denardim/research/NBERwp33874.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PDF</a> · <a href="https://users.nber.org/~denardim/research/BDYT_75min_May_2025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Slides</a></div>
    <div class="paper-abstract"><strong>Abstract.</strong> We quantify why most households save and work, using a life-cycle model with wage risk, endogenous labor supply of both spouses, marital transitions, health, medical expenses, mortality, and bequest motives at the death of the first and last household member, estimated on PSID and HRS data. A model with more motives to save and endogenous labor supply implies smaller roles for precautionary saving against wage risk and for bequest motives than previously suggested. Modeling the entire life cycle implies a larger role for medical expenses, because households save and work for them long before they retire. Marriage and divorce risk has large and offsetting effects on the savings of couples and singles, and thus small effects on aggregate savings, but affects aggregate earnings as much as wage risk.</div>
  </article>
